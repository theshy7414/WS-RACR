WS-RACR

World-State-Centered Retrieval-Augmented Collaboration Reasoning for Human–Robot Collaboration

An LLM-based collaboration system for human–robot assembly on a Techman TM5-900 arm. Retrieved historical plans are treated as candidates and verified against the current world state before the robot decides to reuse, adapt, clarify or reject them.

Source code Email

The problem

Natural-language HRC systems built on LLMs and RAG tend to fail in three ways:

  • Ambiguous, multi-intent commands: one utterance can mix an action, a question and an unclear request.
  • Stale plans: a retrieved plan may no longer match the scene (a part is missing, the gripper is occupied, the human said stop).
  • Similar but incompatible plans: similarity-based retrieval can mark a plan with a different action type as directly reusable.

Approach

The robot reasons over a shared world state Wt = {St, Ot, Ht, Rt} (task step, objects, human, robot), updated by an asynchronous perception pipeline. Retrieval only recalls candidates; three decision layers choose the outcome.

Results

Four experiments target each design decision: (EXP1) intent routing on 100 utterances, (EXP2) feasibility gating on 60 plan-reuse cases, (EXP3) action-type risk on 80 conflict pairs, and (EXP4) end-to-end ablation on 44 stratified scenarios. Reproduce with python -m evaluation.run_all.

Intent routing (EXP1, 100 utterances)

Layer 1 is evaluated on 100 annotated utterances spanning six groups: pure EXECUTE (A, n=30), pure ANSWER (B, n=20), clarification-needed (C, n=10), EXECUTE+ANSWER (D, n=20), ANSWER+EXECUTE (E, n=10), and EXECUTE+EXECUTE (F, n=10). Groups D–F are compound multi-intent: utterance-level methods that return a single label fail entirely on all multi-intent groups (n=40). “Multi-intent” = groups D+E+F (n=40), where all intents must be returned in order.

The table shows that utterance-level and LLM single-prompt methods fail entirely on compound commands (0% multi-intent accuracy) because they produce at most one intent label. LLM multi-step achieves 85% overall but still misses 23% of compound cases. Layer 1 segment parsing reaches 92% overall and 93% on multi-intent groups, with ~0.005 ms rule-parsing time (local micro-benchmark) vs. an estimated ~850–950 ms for LLM-based routing (nominal estimate, not an end-to-end measurement).

End-to-end ablation (EXP4, 44 stratified scenarios)

The purpose of this evaluation is controlled failure-mode isolation rather than dataset-scale benchmarking. The 44 cases are stratified to isolate each layer: 12 ambiguous commands (Cat-A, Layer 1 stress), 10 plan-reuse cases with feasibility variation (Cat-B: 6 object-missing REJECTs + 4 feasible controls, Layer 2 stress), 12 execution-risk pairs (Cat-C, Layer 3 stress), and 10 routine instructions (Cat-D). Acc = end-to-end accuracy over all 44 cases; Rout.Err, HRR, and FRR are subset-specific rates computed on their corresponding stress categories (Cat-A, Cat-B, Cat-C respectively).

The results show a near block-diagonal improvement pattern: each layer primarily improves its intended failure category. Layer 1 raises Cat-A from 8.3% to 83.3%, Layer 2 raises Cat-B from 20% to 80% (HRR 60% → 0%), and Layer 3 raises Cat-C from 41.7% to 100% (FRR 58% → 0%). The three remaining errors comprise ≈2 semantically similar multi-intent utterances missed by keyword splitting (Cat-A) and one Cat-D case with STT-induced character errors.
